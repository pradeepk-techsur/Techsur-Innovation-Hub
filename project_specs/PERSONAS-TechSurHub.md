## PER-01: Margaret Holloway

**Role & Context:**
Margaret is a TSIO division director with oversight of multiple technology programs across the Administrative Office. She participates in investment review boards, sponsors emerging-technology evaluations, and is frequently asked to weigh in on whether a proof of concept warrants further funding or operational exploration. Her time is constrained — she receives briefings, reviews short decision documents, and delegates deep technical review to her team. She does not attend POC demonstrations unless she has already decided the work is relevant to her portfolio.

Margaret's current experience with I&R outputs is entirely dependent on whether someone on her staff happened to hear about the work and thought to forward it to her. She has no reliable way to discover what I&R has explored, what was found, or whether any of it maps to a problem her programs are actively facing. When she needs to make a sponsorship decision, she wants a single, credible, governance-visible view of the work — not a raw technical document or a marketing summary that hides constraints.

**Goals:**
- Discover whether I&R has explored problems relevant to her portfolio without requiring a prior contact or a project name (F1, F2)
- Understand what evidence was produced, what the main findings are, and what the associated risks and constraints are (F3.3, F3.4, F3.5)
- Distinguish an early-stage experiment from a production-validated pattern, so she can calibrate the level of sponsorship or evaluation appropriate to the work (F3.5, F4.2, F1.6)
- Identify who owns the next step and how to initiate a formal discussion, demonstration, or evaluation (F3.9, F8)
- Receive an executive perspective that is grounded in the same factual record as the technical perspective — not a separate narrative that could conflict (F4.1, F4.2, F4.4)

**Pain Points:**
- Innovation outputs are invisible unless a staff member already knows the project name, team, or SharePoint location — discovery depends entirely on informal networks (PRD §2)
- There is no way to distinguish a promising POC from a stalled experiment without reading raw technical documents she does not have time to parse (PRD §2)
- Engaging I&R requires already knowing who to contact and for what — there is no governed path to request a briefing or assessment (PRD §2)
- Executive summaries, when they exist, are often produced as separate documents that may drift from or omit constraints the technical record contains (F4)
- Institutional learning from past POCs does not compound — each decision cycle starts with limited visibility into what came before (PRD §2)

**Key Questions She Brings to the Hub:**
- What problem does this address, and why does it matter for the Judiciary?
- What evidence was actually produced — not what was hoped for?
- What are the main risks, constraints, and production-readiness gaps?
- How mature is this, and what decision or sponsorship is needed to move it forward?
- Who would own the next step, and what would I be committing to as a sponsor?

**Technical Expertise:** Low-to-Intermediate — comfortable reading structured decision documents and high-level architecture summaries; does not evaluate code or infrastructure details directly

**Top Tasks:**
1. Browse the catalog or search by mission area to identify innovation work relevant to her programs (frequent, critical — entry point for all downstream decisions)
2. Open an innovation record and read the executive perspective to assess relevance, evidence quality, and credibility (frequent, critical)
3. Review maturity, review status, key risks, and ownership to determine the appropriate level of investment or engagement (frequent, high)
4. Initiate an engagement request — demo, adoption discussion, or related briefing — from the record page (as-needed, high)
5. Share a relevant record with a staff member for deeper technical evaluation (as-needed, medium)

**Success Criteria:**
- Can locate a relevant innovation record using mission or problem language without knowing the project name or team (PRD §11)
- Can identify the problem, outcome, evidence, maturity, review context, risks, constraints, owner, and recommended next step from a single record in under 10 minutes
- Receives an executive perspective that reflects the same underlying evidence and maturity as the technical perspective — no conflicting versions
- Can initiate an engagement request that reaches I&R with sufficient context for follow-up, without sending a separate email to locate the right contact

## PER-02: David Tran

**Role & Context:**
David is a program manager and application owner in a district court technology office. He is responsible for a portfolio of applications serving court operations and is frequently asked by leadership whether emerging capabilities could address operational bottlenecks in his workflows. He is attentive to what other offices and the AO are working on, but he has no systematic way to discover whether I&R has explored something relevant to his current problems.

David evaluates innovation work through an operational lens: he cares whether something would work in his environment, what it would cost his office in staffing and infrastructure, what responsibilities his team would need to assume, and whether I&R is looking for an adopter or a collaborator. He is not opposed to engagement but needs enough structured information to prepare a conversation with his leadership before committing any organizational resources.

**Goals:**
- Determine whether innovation work applies to a workflow problem his office is actually experiencing (F1, F2, F3.1, F3.6)
- Understand what adoption would require — including skills, dependencies, infrastructure, and ownership commitments — before initiating any discussion (F3.6, F3.5, F4.2)
- Know whether I&R is actively seeking adopters or collaborators for a given effort (F1.4, F3.9)
- Submit a mission problem or workflow friction to I&R in a structured way that captures enough context for relevance assessment (F6)
- Request a demonstration or adoption discussion with enough contextual information attached that I&R can prepare a useful response (F8)

**Pain Points:**
- There is no governed entry point for discovering what I&R has explored that might apply to operational workflow problems (PRD §2)
- Evaluating applicability requires locating the original project team and reading raw technical artifacts not designed for an operational audience (PRD §2)
- There is no structured path to tell I&R about a problem without it being misconstrued as a request for a specific application (F6.4)
- It is not clear what organizational commitments adoption would require — reuse guidance is informal or absent (PRD §2)
- Attribution of who owns the innovation and who would own the next step is unclear, making it difficult to identify the right conversation partner (F3.7)

**Key Questions He Brings to the Hub:**
- Does this apply to my office or a workflow I currently own?
- What would adoption actually require from my team — skills, infrastructure, operational responsibility?
- What dependencies would we need to stand up or contract for?
- Is I&R looking for an adopter or a collaborator, and how do I begin that conversation?
- What has already been validated, and what would still be our office's problem to solve?

**Technical Expertise:** Intermediate — reads architecture summaries and operational requirements documents comfortably; relies on technical staff for detailed implementation evaluation

**Top Tasks:**
1. Search by problem type or mission area and browse catalog results to identify work relevant to a current operational need (frequent, critical)
2. Open an innovation record and review the executive and reuse guidance perspectives to assess operational applicability (frequent, high)
3. Review ownership, contributing office, and reuse potential to identify who to contact and what engagement type makes sense (frequent, high)
4. Submit an opportunity — describing a workflow problem for I&R consideration — without framing it as a feature request (as-needed, high)
5. Request a demonstration or adoption discussion from a specific innovation record (as-needed, medium)

**Success Criteria:**
- Can determine whether a record applies to an operational need and what adoption would require without relying on a direct I&R contact (PRD §11)
- Can submit a problem with sufficient context for I&R to assess relevance, feasibility, and portfolio fit — and receive confirmation that submission does not imply acceptance
- Can initiate a demonstration or adoption discussion that arrives at I&R with the originating record and request context attached
- Reuse guidance answers the specific question: "What would my office need to own, and what can we realistically reuse?"

## PER-03: Priya Suresh

**Role & Context:**
Priya is a cloud architect and DevSecOps engineer at the AO, assigned to evaluate emerging technology capabilities for potential use in Judiciary infrastructure. She regularly works with teams exploring new approaches to security, platform architecture, and infrastructure automation. When she evaluates an I&R proof of concept, she wants the full technical record — design decisions, tools used, service dependencies, what broke, what the known gaps are, and what the authoritative artifacts are.

Priya is technically sophisticated and skeptical of summaries that minimize limitations. She does not want a polished story about a POC — she wants to understand exactly what was tested, what constraints were discovered, and what would need to change before she could responsibly use any component in a production environment. She frequently reviews findings that have security, cloud-platform, or compliance implications and needs to trace them back to authoritative sources, not Hub summaries.

**Goals:**
- Access a technical perspective on an innovation record that surfaces architecture, tools, security considerations, data flow, testing approach, and known limitations — not a polished executive summary (F4.3, F3.4)
- Identify exactly which components are available for reuse and what production-readiness gaps exist before she can use them (F3.6, F3.5)
- Navigate directly to authoritative artifacts — reports, architecture diagrams, code repositories, test results, security findings — linked from the record (F3.8)
- Filter catalog results by technology, maturity, review status, and artifact availability to narrow to technically substantive records (F2.3)
- Request technical guidance or an adoption discussion from a specific record when she needs to speak directly with the team that built it (F8)

**Pain Points:**
- Technical findings, architecture diagrams, and security constraints are buried in project folders, SharePoint documents, and Git repositories she cannot locate without already knowing the project team (PRD §2)
- Polished POC write-ups frequently omit or minimize production-readiness gaps, testing gaps, and environmental constraints that are critical to technical evaluation (PRD §3 trust model)
- There is no consistent metadata structure across POC outputs — maturity and review status are undefined or conflated, making it impossible to assess governance state at a glance (PRD §6.1, §6.2)
- Artifact access requires knowing multiple separate repository locations and whether she has permissions — there is no single record that links to what exists (F3.8)
- Engaging the original technical team requires navigating informal channels without a structured request context (PRD §2)

**Key Questions She Brings to the Hub:**
- How was this designed, and what tools and services were actually used?
- What code, infrastructure definitions, or architecture artifacts are available and where are they?
- What technical decisions were made, and what were the consequences?
- What is genuinely reusable versus what is environment-specific or proof-of-concept-only?
- What security, data, and cloud constraints apply, and have they been formally reviewed?
- What is the production-readiness gap, and what must change before I can use this?

**Technical Expertise:** Expert — deep experience with cloud infrastructure, security engineering, DevSecOps tooling, and architecture review; reads technical specifications directly

**Top Tasks:**
1. Search by technology area, maturity level, or review status — especially "technically reviewed" or "security reviewed" — and filter to records with available artifacts (frequent, critical)
2. Open an innovation record and navigate directly to the technical perspective: architecture, tools, findings, limitations, and source artifacts (frequent, critical)
3. Inspect reuse guidance to identify what is genuinely portable versus environment-specific (frequent, high)
4. Follow artifact links to authoritative sources — POC reports, architecture diagrams, repositories, test results — for deeper technical review (frequent, high)
5. Request technical guidance from I&R when the record raises questions the artifact links do not answer (as-needed, medium)

**Success Criteria:**
- Can locate authoritative technical artifacts from an innovation record without contacting I&R or searching external repositories (PRD §11)
- Can identify what is technically reusable, what is not, and what the known production-readiness gaps are from a single record
- Can distinguish security-reviewed work from technically reviewed work from unreviewed work at a glance (F3.5, F9.7)
- Can request technical guidance that reaches the right I&R contact with the originating record and specific technical question attached (F8)

## PER-04: Carlos Rivera

**Role & Context:**
Carlos is a technical lead in a federal court unit that recently completed a small infrastructure modernization experiment using cloud-native tooling. His team produced a working prototype, documented their findings, and is now wondering whether their work could be useful to other courts — or whether I&R is exploring overlapping problems he should know about. He has also been asked by a program manager to surface a workflow automation problem to I&R for potential research consideration.

Carlos wears two hats on the Hub: he may arrive to submit an existing innovation contribution (F7), or to file an opportunity submission describing a mission problem his office is facing (F6). In both cases, he needs the process to be clear, attribution-preserving, and honest about what happens next — he does not want his team's work presented as centrally endorsed or production-ready before curation and review.

**Goals:**
- Submit existing innovation work — including artifacts, contributors, and maturity context — through a structured flow that preserves attribution and makes clear that contribution does not imply endorsement (F7)
- Submit a mission problem or workflow friction to I&R in a structured way that does not imply acceptance into the I&R portfolio (F6)
- Understand what happens to a submission after it is filed — curation process, timeline expectation, and how to follow up (F6.4, F7.4)
- Retain clear ownership of his team's work through the curation process, including the ability to identify current owner and authoritative artifact locations (F7.3, F3.7)
- Discover whether I&R has already explored problems related to what his team is working on, to avoid duplication (F1, F2)

**Pain Points:**
- There is no structured path to share innovation work without it being misrepresented as endorsed, production-ready, or centrally owned (PRD §3 trust model)
- Submitting a problem to I&R currently requires knowing who to contact, how to frame a research ask versus a feature request, and how to write up sufficient context — none of which is documented (PRD §2)
- Attribution and ownership of contributed work are frequently lost in informal sharing or document migration (F7.3)
- There is no way to discover whether related work already exists in I&R's portfolio, so teams may duplicate effort unknowingly (PRD §2)
- The distinction between submitting an opportunity (a problem for I&R to investigate) and sharing existing innovation (work already done) is not clear in any current channel (F6, F7)

**Key Questions He Brings to the Hub:**
- Is there a structured way to share what my team built with other courts or offices?
- How do I describe a problem to I&R without it becoming a feature request or implying I expect them to build something?
- Will my team's attribution be preserved if this work is published?
- What happens after I submit — is there a curation process, and how will I know?
- Has I&R already explored something related to what we're working on?

**Technical Expertise:** Intermediate-to-Expert — comfortable with cloud infrastructure, infrastructure-as-code, and technical documentation; familiar with submission and contribution workflows in technical contexts

**Top Tasks:**
1. Search or browse the catalog to check whether I&R has existing work related to the problem his team is solving (as-needed, high)
2. Submit existing innovation work through the contribution flow — including artifacts, contributors, maturity, and collaboration preference (as-needed, critical)
3. Submit a mission problem or workflow friction through the opportunity submission flow — framed around the problem, not a requested solution (as-needed, high)
4. Review submission confirmation and understand the curation process and next steps (once per submission, high)
5. Contact I&R through an engagement request to discuss collaboration or related innovation work (as-needed, medium)

**Success Criteria:**
- Can submit existing innovation work with attribution, artifact links, and maturity context intact — and receive confirmation that the work will be curated before any publication
- Can submit a problem for I&R consideration in a structured way that clearly states submission does not imply acceptance
- Can verify from the submission confirmation that attribution and current ownership are captured correctly
- Can discover whether related I&R work exists before duplicating effort, using problem-oriented search (PRD §11)

## PER-05: Jasmine Okafor

**Role & Context:**
Jasmine is an authorized I&R team member responsible for the quality, lifecycle, and structured presentation of all content on the Innovation Hub. She is the primary operator of the curation and administration interface (F9), moving innovation records from raw source material through draft, review, and publication — and managing the full lifecycle through supersession, archiving, and retirement. She works with source documents, POC reports, lessons-learned artifacts, and stakeholder submissions, translating them into structured innovation records that meet the Hub's publication gate requirements.

Jasmine is also the triage point for incoming opportunity submissions (F6) and innovation contributions (F7), and she reviews engagement activity (F8) to ensure I&R can follow up on stakeholder requests. She is responsible for maintaining audit history, applying consistent governance metadata, and making sure the Hub's trust indicators — maturity, review status, last-reviewed date, and disclaimers — are accurate and visible. She is the person most affected by any ambiguity in the content model or publication requirements, and she needs clear in-product reference material to apply governance consistently across records.

**Goals:**
- Create, edit, review, publish, supersede, archive, and retire innovation records through a governed lifecycle interface with publication gate enforcement (F9.3, F9.4, F9.9, F9.10)
- Apply consistent metadata — maturity, review status, attribution, ownership, artifact links — using clear in-product reference material so governance is not interpretation-dependent (F9.16, F9.6, F9.7)
- Review incoming opportunity submissions and innovation contributions, disposition them, and initiate record creation from accepted contributions (F9.12, F9.13)
- Monitor engagement activity associated with each record and record follow-up status so I&R can respond to stakeholder requests (F9.14)
- Configure routing destinations and approved settings without requiring a code change or redeployment (F9.15)
- Maintain a complete, auditable history of material changes to content, governance, lifecycle, and configuration (F9.11)

**Pain Points:**
- Innovation outputs currently live across SharePoint, Git repos, project folders, and individual team knowledge — there is no consistent structured format to work from when creating records (PRD §2)
- Maturity and review status are currently informal, inconsistently applied, or conflated — making it difficult to apply governance labels with confidence (PRD §6.1, §6.2)
- Engagement with I&R arrives through informal channels without standard context fields, making it hard to associate a request with a specific innovation effort or determine next steps (PRD §2, F8)
- Lifecycle changes — supersession, archiving, retirement — are not systematically tracked, so stale content can persist without clear signals to stakeholders (PRD §6.4)
- Content model definitions (maturity stages, review status values, publication gate requirements) are documented outside the product, requiring curators to consult external references to apply governance consistently (F9.16)

**Key Questions She Brings to the Hub:**
- What records need review, update, or lifecycle action right now?
- Does this record meet the publication gate — does it have a problem statement, owner, maturity, review status, attribution, source basis, last-reviewed date, and applicable disclaimer?
- What review statuses have been applied, and what is still outstanding before this record can be validated for reuse?
- What opportunity submissions and contributions are in the queue, and which warrant record creation?
- What engagement has occurred on this record, and has I&R followed up appropriately?
- What changed in this record's history, and who made the change?

**Technical Expertise:** Intermediate — comfortable with structured web-based admin interfaces, metadata management, and document curation workflows; does not require engineering skills but benefits from clear in-product governance reference material

**Top Tasks:**
1. Review the curator summary dashboard to identify records needing action, new submissions, and pending engagement (daily, critical)
2. Create or edit an innovation record from source material, applying full content model, metadata, maturity, review status, attribution, and artifact links (frequent, critical)
3. Move a record through the publication lifecycle — draft to review to publish, or publish to supersede/archive/retire — enforcing the publication gate at each transition (frequent, critical)
4. Triage incoming opportunity submissions and innovation contributions, disposition them, and initiate record creation from accepted contributions (as-needed, high)
5. Review engagement activity on published records and record follow-up status for I&R tracking (as-needed, high)
6. Update settings — routing destination, approved configuration — without code deployment (as-needed, medium)

**Success Criteria:**
- Can create a complete, publication-gate-compliant innovation record from source material without consulting external documentation (F9.3, F9.10, F9.16)
- Can move a record through the full lifecycle — draft → review → publish → supersede/archive/retire — with all required fields enforced and all material changes recorded in audit history (F9.9, F9.11)
- Can review and disposition all queued submissions and contributions, with clear workflow state for each (F9.12, F9.13)
- Can review engagement activity and record follow-up status for every published record (F9.14)
- Can update the engagement routing destination without requiring a code change or redeployment (F9.15)
- Zero published records missing required trust fields (maturity, review status, owner, last-reviewed date, disclaimer) — publication gate enforcement prevents this at the source (F9.10)
